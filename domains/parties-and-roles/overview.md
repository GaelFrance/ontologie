# Parties, roles, clients and accounts: an introduction

## What this domain covers and why it matters

Every question the business asks starts with "who": who is the client, who executed the order, who clears it, who owes the fee, whose position is this? This domain gives one shared answer. It describes the people and organisations involved (parties), the functions they hold toward each other (roles), how a provider groups its clients commercially, how clients are classified, and the accounts in which their activity is recorded. If this layer is wrong, revenue goes to the wrong client or a give-up lands in the wrong account.

## Who is involved

The firm acts through several legal entities and branches. Its clients are funds and their managers, banks, brokers, corporates and trading firms. Around them sit other brokers, trading venues, central counterparties (CCPs) and regulators.

## How things flow in practice

A legal entity is onboarded as a client of one of the firm's legal entities for a given service, for example execute-and-clear on listed futures. It signs agreements, receives regulatory categorisations, and gets one or more client accounts in the clearing broker's books. Its investment manager sends orders and allocations on its behalf. The firm executes on a venue, either as a member of that venue or through another member. The trade is cleared at a CCP in an account opened in the clearing member's name, which the firm maps to the client's own account. For sales and revenue, the client is grouped with related parties into a commercial group.

## Ideas to keep apart

1. **A party is not a role.** A bank is a party; "client", "clearing member" and "executing broker" are roles it holds toward someone, in a service, for a period. One bank can hold all of them at once.
2. **The contractual client is not the person who instructs it, nor the party that bears the result.** A fund is the client; its investment manager sends the orders; the investors behind an omnibus account bear the gains and losses.
3. **There are several kinds of group.** The commercial group is the firm's own choice for coverage and revenue. The ownership group follows legal parents. Regulators use their own consolidation groups. They often look similar and must never be swapped.
4. **Membership is not access.** Being a member of a CCP or venue is a role granted under a membership category. The arrangement that grants it, or that gives access through another member, is a separate agreement.
5. **Clearing broker, clearing member and FCM differ.** The first is the service toward the client, the second is admission at a CCP, the third is a US registration.
6. **An account is not its identifier.** A give-up reference points to another firm's account; it is not an account of its own.
7. **A CCP account is not a client account.** The CCP keeps one; the clearing broker keeps the other; an explicit, dated mapping links them.
8. **Some classifications belong to the legal entity, others to the relationship.** EU clearing-obligation status belongs to the entity; the MiFID client category belongs to the relationship with one provider.

## What is deliberately elsewhere

The CCP account, clearing member, clearing broker and client clearing agreement are described with clearing services and accounts. Executing brokers and venue members belong with execution. The arrangements that grant access are on the service offering and market access page. Regulatory regimes belong to regulation; revenue attribution to fees and revenue. Know-your-customer checks are outside this ontology for now.
