# Regulation and rule changes: an introduction

## What this domain covers

Derivatives execution and clearing run on rules written by others: regulators write laws and technical standards; CCPs and exchanges write rulebooks, contract terms, fee lists and margin models. All of these change constantly: a CCP raises a haircut, an exchange moves a block trade threshold, a regulator consults on a new obligation. This domain describes those rules, how they change, and the regulatory statuses that decide who must do what.

Any change can touch a service the business sells, a process operations runs, a cost clients pay, or a document clients signed. The first question is always: "this was published; what could it affect, and by when?"

## Who is involved

- **Lawmakers and supervisors**: EU legislators, the Commission, ESMA and national authorities; the UK Parliament, Bank of England and FCA; the US CFTC and SEC; each Asia-Pacific regulator.
- **Market infrastructures**: CCPs and exchanges, which issue rulebooks and circulars, often after approval by their own supervisor.
- **Industry associations**, which publish readings and standard documents but make no rules.
- **Inside the firm**: Regulatory Affairs (owner of this domain), Legal, Compliance, business management and the product owners who assess impact.

## How it flows in practice

A rule exists in a published version. Someone proposes a change, sometimes through a public consultation. The change is adopted, sometimes approved or self-certified with a supervisor, then enters into force, then applies, often in phases by type of firm or product. Each step comes with a document: a consultation paper, a final act, a circular or a notice. One circular may announce several changes with different dates.

The firm records each change, assesses what it affects (a clearing service, an account type, a margin model, a give-up deadline, a contract) and decides whether to act, plan or ignore it. Readings that need judgement are signed off by Regulatory Affairs and business management, with Legal and Compliance.

Separately, regimes give statuses: a CCP is recognised, a venue is a regulated market, a client is above a clearing threshold, a firm is registered as a futures commission merchant. Obligations, such as the duty to clear certain swaps, attach to those statuses.

## Ideas to hold apart

1. **A regime and a rulebook.** Law of a jurisdiction versus the rules of one CCP or exchange.
2. **The rule, the change and the notice.** What the rule says, the move from one version to the next, and the document that announces it.
3. **Proposed, adopted, in force and applicable.** A consultation is not law; a published rule may not yet apply.
4. **Dates by role.** Publication, entry into force, application, phase-in and end of transition are different dates.
5. **A status, an obligation and its application.** Being above a threshold, the duty that follows, and whether that duty applies to a given client.
6. **Authorised, member, service provider.** A regulatory registration, admission by a CCP and the service given to a client are three things.
7. **Eligible, mandatory, offered.** What a CCP accepts, what the law forces, and what the firm sells.
8. **Fact and interpretation.** The text of a rule versus someone's reading of it.

## What is deliberately elsewhere

Contract specifications, calendars, cut-offs, margin models, eligibility lists, fee schedules, segregation models and account types are defined in their own domains; this domain only records that a rule change touches them. Which rules apply to the firm and its clients, its own procedures and its impact assessments are internal and never written in the shared ontology. Regulatory reporting is not yet developed.
