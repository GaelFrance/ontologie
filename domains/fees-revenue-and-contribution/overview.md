# Fees, revenue and contribution: an introduction

## What this domain covers

This domain describes what is charged for executing and clearing listed and OTC derivatives, who owes it to whom, and how those charges become the management figures the business is steered by: revenue per client, per service and per channel, and contribution after direct costs.

It matters because the same trade can carry five or six different charges flowing in different directions, and a single loose word ("commission", "execution fee", "brokerage") can double-count money or hide a cost. Business management needs to know what each client and each service really brings in, and that answer is only as good as the meanings underneath it.

## Who is involved

- The **client**, who pays the firm for execution, clearing or both, and sometimes pays other brokers through the firm.
- The **firm** in its roles: executing broker, clearing broker, or a client of another broker when it uses a third party.
- **Trading venues** and **CCPs**, which publish fee schedules and charge their members; one may collect the other's fees.
- **Other executing brokers**, paid executing brokerage under a give-up agreement.
- **Regulators and self-regulatory bodies**, which levy some transaction fees in certain regimes.
- Inside the firm: the revenue owner, business finance, the product owners and the collateral manager, who owns collateral income.

## How things flow in practice

Every charge starts from a fee schedule: a venue's or CCP's published price list, or the pricing terms agreed with a client. When something chargeable happens (an execution, a registration at the CCP, a give-up, an exercise, a month of keeping an OTC trade open), a charge accrues. Some charges are passed on to the client at cost or with a mark-up; others stay with the firm as a cost. Charges are then invoiced or debited on a statement, may be disputed, corrected, waived or reduced by a rebate, and are finally settled in cash, not always by the party that owed them.

Management revenue is built from these charges, plus other income such as interest earned on client cash collateral, under a stated reporting basis: whose books, which period, which currency, gross or net of pass-through. Contribution then deducts the costs that an approved cost perimeter includes.

## Ideas to hold apart

1. **A charge, revenue and cash** are three different things, recorded at different moments.
2. **Owing, paying and collecting**: the debtor, the payer and the collector of a charge can be three different parties.
3. **Our fee and their fee passed on**: a client fee is not the venue or CCP fee it may include.
4. **All-in and fees-plus pricing**: compare like with like.
5. **Brokerage received and brokerage paid**: the same word from opposite sides.
6. **Discount, rebate, waiver and adjustment**: four different ways a charge ends up lower.
7. **Revenue, contribution and profitability**: each deducts more than the last; none has an approved formula yet.
8. **Fee income and collateral income**: separate streams with separate owners.

## What is deliberately elsewhere

The events that give rise to charges (executions, give-ups, registrations, transfers) are described in the execution and clearing domains. The service a client buys is described with the service offering. Cash payments, reconciliations and breaks belong to post-trade operations. Margin, collateral and price alignment belong to margin and collateral. Disclosure duties on costs and charges belong to regulation. How the firm actually prices its clients, which costs it deducts and how it presents pass-through are firm facts: they are captured through the company questions, never written into the definitions.
